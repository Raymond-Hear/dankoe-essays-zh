1) Brand - How you present yourself or your business to the world.

In your personal life, when people think of your name, what do they associate with you? Do they think you’re cool? Fun to be around? Helpful and trustworthy?

Brand is that, but for your business. Like a digital storefront.

This is how marketers made 10x more than indie programmers in the past, because they gave people what they wanted.

As a one-person business, in today’s world, your brand is probably going to be a personal brand. And even if you aren’t a one-person business, look at Elon Musk, Mr. Beast, and the rest. A personal brand is one of the most effective growth strategies, if you can pull it off.

2) Offer - What people pay you for that helps them get a result.

You need a product or service.

That’s obvious... you can’t make money without one.

But an offer is the perceived value of that product or service. It’s how you package it. It’s the deal. This is one of the most profitable skills you can learn, because once you nail the offer, the landing page, copywriting, marketing, and the rest become that much easier.

That’s what you compete on. Not the product you’re selling, the deal you’re offering. The amount of value compared to price.

Offer creation is the art of pulling 4 levers.

  1. The dream outcome - what the customer actually wants

  2. The delivery mechanism - how you get them there (course, coaching, supplement, etc)

  3. The speed of results - how fast you get them there

  4. The guarantee - what happens if it fails

How to create your own AI team (the best way to use AI in general)

This section is going to lose quite a few people.

Because even though we’re talking about AI, it’s a difficult process in a world of cheap dopamine seekers. It takes a certain level of systems thinking to understand and implement.

The problem with AI is that people think of it as some kind of all powerful being.

And since it’s not an all powerful being, when it doesn’t do exactly what they want (because they don’t know what they want), they just assume it’s only capable of generating slop.

Yesterday, I saw some guy was begging businesses to not create their website with ChatGPT or AI because it “looks bad” and you can tell it’s slop.

If you actually use AI, you find this laughable.

It’s true... if you just have it one-shot the entire website and put no effort in beyond that. But if you give it examples and clear instructions, you’re off to at least a better start. After that, you have to start digging into the details. You have to tell it to tweak the border radius, the drop shadows, the padding, the column layout, and everything else you would already do in Figma, Photoshop, Webflow, or Framer. The process hasn’t changed, but the toolbar filled with knobs and numbers has turned into natural language.

Telling AI to “create a website” or “write a newsletter” or “write a viral tweet” today is like using a Wordpress template in the past. People can tell. There was just as much slop in the past as there was today, but AI is some special case that people really dislike.

You can stop there and have a pretty terrible website that does not make your brand stand out in the slightest, or you can use that as a starting point and iterate further, just like you can build an app and resolve the issues for weeks on end until it’s actually a good app.

The baseline for what is considered a modern Wordpress template will continue to rise, forcing people to either develop taste and experience or default to being perceived as average.

This is part of why my opinion around writing with AI is changing, because if you treated it like you did when you actually write, you’d realize you’d have more control, more creativity, and more opportunity to create something truly unique that nobody has even thought to do before. But people say “write this newsletter like me” and expect it to account for the hundreds, if not thousands of decisions they make when actually writing... obviously the AI isn’t just going to pull those out of your head and do exactly what you want, because you didn’t tell it to do that.

It only sounds like AI slop because the person chose to post it in that state, and doesn’t have the taste to notice it or the knowledge to iterate until it becomes something only they could create.

With that out of the way, how do you actually do this?

How can we create something like a weekly content strategist that’s actually good?

1) You need to know what good looks like, or have an example of what good looks like

When it comes to “good” content, there’s a lot at play.

There’s the structure of the post, article, or newsletter. There’s the creator’s vibe or energy signature. There’s the throughline of their brand and big ideas they incorporate consistently to get their worldview across. And there’s the unique ideas they stumble across that help them stand out from everyone else. All of this needs to be accounted for.

No AI is going to tell you that. You can only find it through experience or mentorship from someone who has that specific knowledge (that’s why reading this gives you a slight advantage).

But now that you know you need those things, you need the reference, example, or instructions to give to the AI because it doesn’t know how to do it on it’s own.

That means YouTube titles and transcripts of videos that represent the type of content you want to create. It means reels transcripts, Substack notes or articles, X posts or articles, or ample examples of the style of writing you like.

For web design, this usually just means giving the AI a ton of screenshots as you work through each section of the page you’re creating, or writing out in detail what you want.

That’s how I start almost every AI or agent chat that isn’t just a simple question.

If I know what I want, then I give detailed instructions. I’ve written 2000 word prompts teaching it to write tweets like I do, and it gets extremely close. You wouldn’t be able to do that just by asking it to look at my best tweets, because it will try to templatize those and doesn’t account for the thinking process that went into writing them.

If I don’t know what I want, then I start researching and asking questions until I have clarity and fill the context with the right information.

Meaning, I don’t just say “write me a newsletter.”

I ask questions that help me decide what a good newsletter looks like.

In your case, you could ask something like, “I want to write one of the greatest, most engaging newsletters in the world. Something that stands out and doesn’t sound robotic. Let’s treat this as a brainstorming session until we land on a style that I like. Start by asking clarifying questions on what you need from me to make this happen. We’ll go from there.”

At the end of that conversation, ask it to condense everything into one document.

Now you have a document you can reference in other chats at any time.

2) You need to understand the process that gets results, or start with someone else’s process until you develop your own

For a content strategist, this isn’t as simple as “research top performing posts and give me ideas.” That’s what the slightly above-average person would do. The average person would just ask the AI to write viral posts.

When I think of my own process, it’s something like this:

  • Read books, save ideas, have conversations with friends, listen to long lectures for ideas that nobody is talking about online

  • Pick an idea at the intersection of (1) what I am excited to write about (2) what people will massively benefit from and (3) what is already validated and performing well

  • Brain dump title ideas for the article/script, and find the best ideas that would fit into that piece

  • Consider which long-form structure would be the best to house those ideas and start outlining it

  • From that outline, brainstorm 10-20 short-form content ideas that I can take and run with

This is also considering I have the branding side of things down, which is an entirely different story - that’s what would allow the content strategist to understand my worldview, influences, mission, brand enemy, and more that would further refine the ideas and make them unique to me.

The difficult part is turning that into a process that AI can help with.

It obviously can’t read books for me or discover ideas for me. That’s the edge that can’t be outsourced.

I can capture these ideas in a searchable library so AI can suggest a few options each week. I can then spend more time reading and learning, a process I enjoy.

Then, the main thing I need to do is instruct the AI on how to perform good research, what ideas are worthwhile (celebrity posts that went viral aren’t a signal as to what’s performing on social media. Small to mid-size account’s outlier posts are), how to match the right topics to my own novel ideas, and examples of my own content structures that have worked in the past.

Then you have it spit that out into a table I can work from and you have something like the Weekly Strategist AI that runs on autopilot every week.

3) You need to continuously improve and iterate the system you build, as you’ve always needed to do.

We are building a system here.

And if you’ve ever used a Notion template or had any form of morning routine... you understand that sometimes a system isn’t always reliable, or you outgrow it, or something throws a wrench in it and it doesn’t work for that specific situation.

But that isn’t a reason to think that the system doesn’t work altogether. And it doesn’t mean that you abandon it entirely. It means you iterate and make it adaptable.

With AI, this means that whenever you notice something wrong, you tell it what’s wrong. You improve the system. You propose a solution or converse until you decide on a better way of doing it.
